Power Flow & Short-Circuit Analysis

13.8 kV / 480 V Commercial & Industrial Distribution System — PowerWorld Simulator

Project Overview

Completed

Tools & Standards: PowerWorld Simulator, Newton-Raphson Load Flow, Symmetrical Components Fault Analysis, ANSI C84.1, IEEE 141, IEEE 242

I modeled a 13.8 kV to 480 V commercial/industrial distribution network in PowerWorld to get hands-on with load flow, overload behavior, and short-circuit analysis instead of just reading about them. I built the one-line from scratch, ran a baseline load flow, then pushed one of the panels well past its rated load on purpose to watch the transformer and bus voltages fail. From there I fixed it the way a utility engineer actually would — upsizing the transformer, adjusting the LTC tap, and adding local VAR support — and finished by running a bolted 3-phase fault to size the breakers.

System Setup

The network steps down from a 13.8 kV utility interconnect through a delta-wye main transformer into a 480 V main switchboard, which feeds three radial panels: Panel A (lighting/receptacles, 142.5 kW / 46.8 kVAR), Panel B (industrial motors, 191 kW / 118 kVAR), and Panel C (HVAC, 220 kW / 119 kVAR). I picked those three loads specifically because they each behave differently under a fault or overload — Panel B's motor load has the worst power factor of the three, which is exactly why I later stressed that panel.

Baseline Load Flow

With everything solved under normal operating conditions, the system pulled about 0.553 MW and 0.284 Mvar total, and every bus sat right around 0.9998 p.u. — basically flat, which is what you want to see for a healthy baseline before you start breaking things on purpose.

3. Bus voltage table under normal load

  • Main SWBD voltage: 0.9998 p.u. (479.9 V)
  • Total active load: 0.553 MW
  • Total reactive load: 0.284 Mvar
  • All three panels held steady at 0.9998 p.u. with no losses of note

Stress Test: Overloading Panel B

To see what actual failure looks like in PowerWorld rather than on paper, I bumped Panel B's demand up to 3.5 MW / 2.1 Mvar — way beyond what the transformer was rated for. The 1.0 MVA step-down transformer ended up running at 449% of its nameplate capacity, and the heavy reactive current draw dragged every downstream bus below the 0.95 p.u. ANSI C84.1 floor.

4. 449% transformer overload flagged on the canvas

5. Bus voltages collapsing under the overload

  • Why it failed thermally: A 1.0 MVA transformer just can't carry a load that needs 4.49 MVA — it wasn't a marginal overload, it was almost 4.5x rated capacity, which in a real system means relay tripping or worse before it ever gets that far.
  • Why voltage collapsed alongside it: Panel B's load is heavily inductive, so pushing that much reactive current through the upstream impedance caused the voltage drop to compound — it wasn't just the transformer struggling, the whole feeder was starved for VARs.

Fixing It: Transformer Upgrade & Reactive Compensation

Rather than just rolling back the load to make the problem disappear, I fixed it the way I'd actually want to justify to someone reviewing the design. I resized the transformer to 5.0 MVA and set the LTC tap to +2.5% (1.025 p.u.) to give the secondary side a voltage boost, then added a 2.0 Mvar switched capacitor bank directly at Panel B so the reactive power didn't have to travel all the way from the utility source anymore.

6. Mitigated network with local VAR support

7. Bus voltages restored after mitigation

  • Transformer loading dropped back into a safe operating range, well under 80% of the new 5.0 MVA rating
  • Every downstream panel voltage recovered to roughly 0.99 p.u.
  • All PowerWorld system warnings cleared after re-solving the load flow

Short-Circuit Fault Analysis

Once the system was stable again, I still needed real fault current numbers to actually spec the breakers — a healthy load flow doesn't tell you anything about what happens during a fault. I applied a bolted 3-phase symmetrical fault (Zf = 0) directly at Panel B's bus.

8. Fault analysis calculation window in PowerWorld

  • Fault type: 3-phase balanced bolted fault (R = 0, X = 0)
  • Faulted bus voltage: 0.0000 p.u. — full voltage collapse at the fault point, as expected
  • Subtransient fault current: ≈ 1.15 kA RMS

What This Told Me

  • Sizing matters more than it looks on paper: A transformer that's fine for today's load can fail badly the moment a facility adds motor load without anyone re-checking the math — 449% overload isn't a rounding error, it's a completely undersized system.
  • Reactive power should be fixed locally, not upstream: Adding the capacitor bank right at Panel B did more for voltage stability than the tap change alone — it stopped the inductive current from ever having to travel through the feeder in the first place.
  • Breaker selection has to be based on fault current, not load current: Based on the ≈1.15 kA subtransient fault current, I'd spec MCCBs or power circuit breakers rated at least 10 kA AIC so there's real margin for downstream protective coordination.
